# Screenplay and direction

## Screenwriter

**Novice error:** adapting chronologically and cutting into episodes, so nothing builds.

- **Design from the turn backward.** Find the protagonist's point of no return; build
  toward it. Each episode asks one dramatic question and answers it (or answers it wrong).
- **Every scene turns.** Following McKee, a scene changes a value: the **power ledger**
  (who holds power at start and end). No change → cut or merge.
- **Write for picture.** No dialogue track: story lives in behavior (sitting and standing,
  doors, waiting, an object changing hands, a light off). Intertitles ≤8 words, ≤1 per 30 s.
- **Start late, leave early.**

## Director

- **One "about" sentence per episode** (a theme): "Kindness is a risk worth taking."
  Every choice is checked against it; a beat that doesn't serve it goes.
- **Prove tone on one scene** before multiplying it (tone test).
- **Rhythm from the references' shot lengths**, not habit. 1940s features averaged
  ~9–10 s shots, many 2000s features ~3–5 s (Salt, Bordwell; verify titles on Cinemetrics);
  comedy and action cut faster than drama. Write typical shot and hold length in the bible.
- **Staging power**: height, distance, who sits, who waits, who faces camera. In drama the
  powerful usually move least; in comedy whoever can't keep still is usually losing.
- **One set piece per episode**, planned first, given the most care.

## Motion scene structure

Motion scenes are one subject, one action. A dramatic scene becomes 2–5:
1. *Establish* (2–4 s): where we are, what kind of world; sound bed starts.
2. *Approach* (3–8 s): the subject enters or is revealed.
3. *Turn* (4–12 s): power changes hands; the hold lives here.
4. *Aftermath* (2–6 s): the new state, held.

First frame is the thumbnail: never black; open composed, with title or setting readable.
